10 source-linked stories · September 21, 2026

The day’s most useful signal is that “frontier” advantage is splitting into three separate contests—model efficiency, harness/product design, and permissioned access to real systems—while security and policy are starting to formalize around those boundaries.

TOP 10 · Original release: September 20, 2026

Step 5 Preview pushes agent capability onto a lower-cost sparse frontier

StepFun released Step 5 Preview, a 600B-total/27B-active sparse MoE model with a one-million-token context window and vision input. The company reports 44 on Artificial Analysis’s Intelligence Index, 67.7% on DeepSWE v1.1, and 49.0% on its new 553-repository StepCodeBench.

TOP 10 · Original publication: September 21, 2026

“Frontier Overhangs” argues that model capability is becoming separable from product advantage

Ben Thompson’s new Stratechery essay separates four “overhangs” behind frontier-lab strategy: capability, product, pricing, and capital. Its central technical/business claim is that model-plus-harness integration mattered while agents were not good enough, but improving models and multi-model harnesses are now making the model layer more modular.

OpenAI called for interoperable national and international frontier-AI standards covering measurements, incident reporting, increasing autonomy in AI research, and recursive self-improvement. Amazon blocks Meta Muse, turning agent identity and merchant consent into product constraints

Amazon cut off Meta’s Muse personal agent from shopping on Amazon.com, saying Muse did not identify itself, Meta had not sought participation, and credential handling created privacy and security risks. Meta says credentials enter secure storage invisible to Muse and that a separate Sentinel agent approves outbound actions.

The first industry-wide humanoid tally shows deployment far behind the hype

The International Federation of Robotics’ first humanoid-specific tally found about 7,000 robots sold worldwide in 2025 for industrial and professional-service use. Many were bought for research or training-data generation rather than productive work; automaker pilots commonly involved single- or double-digit fleets.
